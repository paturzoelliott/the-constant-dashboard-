WHAT IS CHART C?

THE CONSTANT uses “Chart C” as shorthand for the single-person pension income-test cessation point. It is the level of assessable fortnightly income at which entitlement under the standard pension income test reaches zero. It is not a poverty line or minimum-income guarantee.

Under the current standard rules, the first $226 per fortnight is income-free and pension entitlement is then reduced by 50 cents per additional dollar. Services Australia publishes the standard single-person cut-off at $2,701.40 per fortnight from 20 September 2026.

THE CONSTANT converts that fortnightly cut-off to a weekly equivalent and compares it with the National Minimum Wage. The comparison does not assume the two measures were legislated to be equal. The research question is how their historical relationship behaved over time and why it changed sharply around 2000.

Chart C can move because of changes in pension rates, the income free area, taper rules and pension structure. THE CONSTANT therefore reconstructs the underlying policy parameters rather than treating Chart C as a simple price index.

EXPLORE THE CONSTANT PROPOSITION ▾
OFFICIAL DERIVED MODELLED PROPOSED

Why Chart C is valuable

Chart C is THE CONSTANT's shorthand for the standard single-person pension income-test cessation point. It is not a poverty line. It is an administrative income boundary created by the interaction of the pension payment rate, income-free area and taper.

That makes it analytically valuable because it belongs to Australia's income-support architecture rather than being only a price index. Its movement can reflect pension indexation, income-test design and policy structure.

THE CONSTANT therefore reconstructs its underlying parameters rather than assuming Chart C simply measures inflation.

The 100% proposition

PROPOSED THE CONSTANT proposes testing an integrated income architecture in which weekly Chart C becomes the 100% reference benchmark and the National Minimum Wage is benchmarked at 100% of weekly Chart C.

Other payments and statutory wage categories would not necessarily equal Chart C. Pensions, JobSeeker, apprentices, trainees, supported wages and other provisions could instead be expressed as transparent policy percentages of the same central benchmark.

The objective is not to remove policy discretion. It is to ensure that changes in one part of Australia's income architecture remain visible to the others.

One benchmark — many connected rates

Under the proposition:

Chart C = 100% reference point
National Minimum Wage = 100% Chart C benchmark
Other statutory payments and wage rates = explicit policy percentages of the same reference

This could provide a transparent common anchor for minimum wages, pensions, unemployment support, apprentice and trainee arrangements, supported wages and other income provisions while still allowing different statutory percentages where policy requires them.

Household shock-absorption capacity

MODELLED THE CONSTANT tests whether a larger margin after essential expenditure could give households greater capacity to absorb increases in rent, food, electricity, gas, fuel, transport, insurance, health costs and other economic shocks.

The expanded LECI/LECIB panels express this as: income → essential costs → income remaining after essential costs.

The hypothesis is that households with a larger remaining income margin may be less vulnerable to sudden price and living-cost shocks than households whose essential costs consume most of their income.

Business taxation and economic reform

PROPOSED / TO BE TESTED THE CONSTANT does not claim that a Chart-C-aligned wage automatically finances reductions in business taxation.

Instead, it proposes testing the full system. A higher wage base may change PAYG receipts, GST-bearing consumption, superannuation contributions, payroll-related revenue, transfer reliance, workers' compensation contributions and other fiscal flows. Those effects must be measured against the additional wage and employment costs faced by employers.

Only after both sides are modelled should the research ask whether any net fiscal improvement could create scope to reduce taxes, charges or other costs imposed on employment and business.

Economic resilience

The larger proposition is that a transparent income benchmark could increase the capacity of households and the wider economy to respond to shocks without requiring every adjustment to be made independently through wages, transfers, concessions, emergency relief or ad-hoc fiscal measures.

That proposition remains an empirical question. THE CONSTANT Live therefore tracks inflation, living costs, unemployment, productivity, wage decisions, pension indexation, government reviews and the fiscal consequences of the alternative wage benchmark.

What THE CONSTANT does not claim

The historical relationship does not by itself prove that Chart C caused minimum-wage outcomes. Nor does it prove that 100% alignment is the only possible policy response.

Any proposed alignment must be tested against employment, employer costs, inflation, productivity, taxation, government expenditure, distributional effects, social-security design and Australia's existing wage-setting institutions.

How the proposition is tested

THE CONSTANT Live deliberately separates four evidence categories:

OFFICIAL figures directly published by government institutions.

DERIVED arithmetic calculated directly from official policy parameters.

MODELLED counterfactual estimates and analytical scenarios.

PROPOSED policy architecture that remains open to independent testing, criticism and alternative modelling.

THE CONSTANT does not ask Australians to accept the proposition. It provides the data needed to test it.
